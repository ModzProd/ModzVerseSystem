Lucha MEX, the only promotion practicing Traditional, has structural access to the largest venue pool in the world but faces the lowest average fan engagement.

Welcome to the ModzVerse

The ModzVerse is a custom professional wrestling world designed for use with the Pro Wrestling Sim booking simulation engine. It is currently in BETA — the foundational layer of the world is complete: the six flagship promotions are established, their calendars are populated, the regional popularity of every continent has been mapped, and the championship structure is in place. What is missing, deliberately, is the human layer — the wrestlers, the contracts, the venues, the broadcast deals — all of which will be populated as the world grows into its first playable season in January 2025.

This wiki is the canonical reference for the world of ModzVerse. It is written in-universe, meaning that every promotion, event, and brand is described as if ModzVerse were a real, ongoing wrestling world with its own history, rivalries, and culture. Where the database provides hard facts (debut dates, prestige ratings, style classifications, regional popularity scores), those facts are treated as historical record. Where the world is still being written — most notably, the worker rosters — the wiki marks those sections as TBD or BETA and explains what is intended to fill them.

The world's tagline, "A World at War!", is not just marketing copy. It is the structural premise of the entire ModzVerse project. Every promotion in the world is positioned in some form of conflict: internal brand warfare (RAIBARAZU's Doragon vs. Fenikkusu), inter-promotional rivalry (the three-way Lucha conflict between Lucha Bellas, Lucha MEX, and their shared Mexican fanbase), geographic isolation (Chicago Show as the lone North American outpost in a Japan/Mexico-dominated world), and the cold war between the two Kings Road women's promotions (Osaka Joshi vs. Valkyrie Project). The wiki's job is to make those conflicts legible.

Featured Promotion
RAIBARAZU — founded June 6, 1950 in Tokyo, Japan, RAIBARAZU is the oldest, largest, and most strategically complex promotion in ModzVerse. With an Asian-region popularity of 60, two competing brands (Doragon and Fenikkusu), three annual supershows, and a year-round touring system feeding each one, it is the beating heart of the ModzVerse calendar. The name RAIBARAZU is a Japanese transliteration of the English word rivals — a name that has come to define the promotion's identity.

World Overview

ModzVerse is a professional wrestling world set to begin its canon timeline on January 1st, 2025. The world is geographically complete — every country on Earth is represented, each with its own population, economy, dictatorship status, and most importantly, its own wrestling popularity score and preferred style. This means that a promoter booking a show in Ontario, Canada (a Strong Style hotbed, popularity 90) is making a fundamentally different decision than booking a show in Cuba (a Traditional style market, popularity 40), and the world's simulation engine will reward or punish them accordingly.

What makes ModzVerse distinct from other wrestling simulations is the asymmetry of its promotion roster. A typical wrestling world is dominated by one or two mega-promotions in the United States. ModzVerse inverts that expectation: there is exactly one American promotion (Chicago Show), and it is explicitly a regional midcard player. The center of gravity in this world is firmly in East Asia (three Japanese promotions) and Latin America (two Mexican promotions). The result is a wrestling landscape where the styles that dominate are Pure Wrestling, Kings Road, Lucha Libre, and Traditional — not the Sports Entertainment style that real-world American wrestling is known for.

Calendar & Era

The ModzVerse canon begins on January 1, 2025. The world is configured with worker generation enabled, meaning the simulation will dynamically create new wrestlers over time to keep rosters fresh as the years progress. Aging effects are not disabled, so wrestlers will age, decline, and eventually retire — a long-term booking consideration. The world is currently in BETA, meaning the structural foundation is in place but the active roster, contracts, venues, and broadcast deals have not yet been written into the database.

Several gameplay toggles are turned off by design: sandbox mode is disabled (the world follows realistic financial constraints), advanced stats are off (the simulation uses the standard skill model rather than the deep-dive 149-column worker schema), advanced popularity mode is off (regional popularity is tracked at the continent level rather than per-region), and owner goals are disabled (the player is not bound to a boss's expectations). These can be toggled on at any time but represent the world's intended default state.

The Three Power Centers

While ModzVerse spans the entire globe, the wrestling world is concentrated in three geographic power centers. North America hosts two of the world's six promotions (Chicago Show, plus the Mexican promotions which are technically classified as North American in the continental popularity system). Asia is by far the dominant region, hosting three promotions — all Japanese — and producing the world's single highest regional popularity score (RAIBARAZU's 60 in Asia). Europe, Africa, South America, and Oceania are unrepresented in the promotion roster; they exist as potential expansion markets, not as active wrestling territories.

This geographic concentration is intentional. The world's three power centers — Chicago, Mexico City, and Tokyo — are positioned to compete for the same global talent pool, while each retaining a distinct regional identity. A worker signed to RAIBARAZU may eventually seek a North American tour with Chicago Show, or a Mexican wrestler may want to test themselves against Japanese Pure Wrestling. The asymmetry creates natural booking pressure: every promotion has gaps that can only be filled by reaching across the ocean.

World Lore: A World at War!

"The world does not lack for wrestling. It lacks for peace." — ModzVerse opening crawl, January 2025

The tagline of the ModzVerse world — "A World at War!" — is not a marketing flourish. It is the structural thesis of the entire project. Every promotion in the world is positioned in active conflict with at least one other entity: a sister promotion, a rival brand, an opposing style, or simply the hostile geography of a region that does not want them. There are no friendly promotions in ModzVerse. There are only promotions that have not yet found a reason to fight.

This section documents the four major conflicts that define the ModzVerse's opening state in January 2025. Each conflict has its own history, its own stakes, and its own vector for escalation over the course of the simulated year. Together, they form the web of tensions that a player booking any of the six promotions will have to navigate.

Conflict One: The Lucha Civil War

The two Mexican promotions — Lucha Bellas and Lucha MEX — share a country, a fanbase, a founding year (1980), and almost nothing else. Lucha Bellas is a women's-exclusive promotion built around the mask-and-flash tradition of Lucha Libre, the high-flying acrobatic style that made Mexican wrestling famous. Lucha MEX is a mixed-gender promotion that practices Traditional wrestling, the old-school territorial style that predates the mask-and-cape era.

The conflict between them is not just stylistic — it is philosophical. Lucha Bellas represents the modern, theatrical, identity-driven side of Mexican wrestling, where the mask is a sacred object and a worker's persona is everything. Lucha MEX represents the older tradition where workrate and storytelling in the ring matter more than the costume a worker wears. Both promotions are based in the same region (México state, population 17 million) and both are fighting for the same fans.

The flashpoint for this conflict is the annual Luchastica supershow, hosted every January Week 5 by Lucha Bellas. Luchastica is positioned as the largest women's wrestling event in the Western Hemisphere, and Lucha MEX has historically resented being overshadowed by a promotion they view as gimmick-driven. The civil war has not yet turned hot — both promotions are currently too small (both have $0 in starting money, both have 0 prestige) — but the structural tension is built in from day one.

Conflict Two: The Japanese Division Wars

Japan hosts three of the world's six promotions — RAIBARAZU, Osaka Joshi, and Valkyrie Project — and they cannot stand each other. This is not one war but two overlapping ones.

The first is RAIBARAZU's internal brand war. RAIBARAZU operates two brands — Doragon (the A-brand, "Dragon") and Fenikkusu (the B-brand, "Phoenix") — that compete for the same roster, the same Tokyo fanbase, and the same top spot on the company's three annual supershows. The name "RAIBARAZU" itself is the Japanese transliteration of the English word rivals, and the company has built its entire identity around the idea that internal competition produces better wrestling. Workers on the Doragon roster are pushed harder, paid more, and given bigger matches; workers on Fenikkusu are positioned as the underdogs, the young lions, the ones fighting to earn a call-up. The result is a constant churn of intra-promotional rivalry that the company monetizes through its three "Road to…" tours.

The second is the cold war between Osaka Joshi and Valkyrie Project. Both are women's-exclusive promotions. Both practice the Kings Road style. Both target the same fanbase. But they could not be more different in trajectory. Osaka Joshi, founded in 2003, has already accumulated 38 prestige points — the only promotion in the world with any — and has $2,983 in the bank, the only profitable Japanese women's promotion. Valkyrie Project, founded in 1980, has zero prestige, zero money, and is the smallest promotion in the world by regional popularity (Asia pop 12, compared to Osaka's 20). Valkyrie Project has the AEGIS LEAGUE championship, the only title in the entire world. Osaka Joshi has nothing but momentum.

This is a textbook asymmetric conflict. Valkyrie Project has the belt; Osaka Joshi has the buzz. Valkyrie Project has history (debuted 23 years earlier); Osaka Joshi has the future. The first time these two promotions share a card — and they will, sooner or later, because there is no one else for either of them to fight — the entire Japanese wrestling scene will have to pick a side.

Conflict Three: The Chicago Anomaly

Chicago Show is, on paper, the most stable promotion in ModzVerse. Founded in 1950 (the joint-oldest promotion in the world alongside RAIBARAZU), it has $50,000 in the bank — more than every other promotion combined. It practices Sports Entertainment, the high-angles, high-drama style that real-world American wrestling is famous for. It runs a weekly event every Sunday. By every financial metric, it should be the dominant promotion in the world.

It is not. Chicago Show has a North American popularity of 40 — a respectable but not dominant number — and zero prestige. It has no brands, no titles, no sister promotions, and no allies. It is the only American promotion in a world that is otherwise overwhelmingly Japanese and Mexican. The "Chicago Anomaly" is the gap between Chicago Show's financial position and its cultural relevance. The promotion is rich but unloved, stable but isolated.

The conflict here is not with another promotion — it is with the entire geography of the wrestling world. Chicago Show's home region (Illinois) is not even a Strong Style or Sports Entertainment hotbed in the regional popularity data; it is overshadowed by Canadian regions like Ontario (pop 90, Strong Style preference) which are technically in the same continent but represent a different fan culture entirely. Chicago Show's war is one of relevance: can a lone American promotion, surrounded by an ocean of Japanese and Mexican influence, find a way to matter?

Conflict Four: The Continental Vacuum

The fourth conflict is the simplest and the most abstract: most of the world has no wrestling at all. Europe, Africa, South America, and Oceania have no home promotions. The 190 countries and 3,218 regions of those continents have wrestling popularity scores — some quite high — but no native promotion to feed that demand. This is the continental vacuum, and it is the long-term strategic prize that every promotion in ModzVerse is implicitly competing for.

A promotion that expands into Europe — even modestly, with a single tour stop — could capture a market that has no incumbent. A worker from Brazil (population 215 million, wrestling popularity 70, Sports Entertainment preference) currently has no home promotion to sign with; they would have to leave their country to wrestle professionally. The first promotion to build an international touring circuit (RAIBARAZU, with its touringCompany flag already enabled, is the obvious candidate) will be positioned to absorb the entire continental vacuum.

This is the slow-burning conflict that will define the ModzVerse's second and third years. The opening year is about the three hot wars — Lucha, Japanese, Chicago. The long war is about who owns the empty map.

History

Founded on New Year's Day, 1950, Chicago Show is the joint-oldest promotion in the ModzVerse (alongside RAIBARAZU, which debuted five months and five days later on June 6 of the same year). The promotion was established in Chicago, Illinois — a city with deep working-class roots and a long tradition of live entertainment — and from the very beginning positioned itself as a Saturday-night institution: a weekly date with the working man, the kind of event you could take your family to after a long week at the stockyards or the factory floor.

The promotion's longevity is its defining trait. In a world where the average promotion has been around for less than fifty years, Chicago Show has been running continuously for seventy-five. That has produced two compounding effects. First, the promotion has accumulated a substantial cash reserve — $50,000 on hand at the start of 2025, more than every other promotion in the world combined. Second, and paradoxically, that longevity has not translated into cultural prestige. Chicago Show enters 2025 with a prestige score of zero, the same as every other promotion in the world except Osaka Joshi. The promotion is rich but unloved, established but not respected.

The reason for this gap is the world itself. Chicago Show is a Sports Entertainment promotion in a wrestling world dominated by Japanese Pure Wrestling, Mexican Lucha Libre, and Japanese Kings Road. The high-angles, high-drama, character-driven style that real-world American promotions built their empires on is, in the ModzVerse, an outlier. Chicago Show's entToWresRatio of 7 means that for every minute of in-ring wrestling, the promotion produces roughly seven minutes of entertainment (angles, promos, backstage segments) — a ratio that would be considered excessive in Tokyo and heretical in Osaka.

Style & Presentation

Chicago Show practices Sports Entertainment, the most angle-heavy of the five styles represented in ModzVerse. The promotion's angleToWresRatio is set to 5 (the world default), but its entToWresRatio of 7 indicates that the entertainment-to-wrestling tilt of its roster is significantly higher than the world average. In practical terms, this means Chicago Show is built around characters and storylines, not workrate. A Chicago Show card is more likely to feature a twenty-minute promo segment than a thirty-minute draw, and the fans who attend a Chicago Show event are there as much for the storyline payoffs as for the matches themselves.

The promotion's impact rating of 6 (out of 10) indicates moderately high emphasis on dramatic moments — finisher kickouts, run-ins, post-match beatdowns. The danger and violence ratings of 5 are both exactly at the world default, meaning Chicago Show is not a hardcore or deathmatch promotion but is willing to use weapons and highspots when the storyline demands it. The promotion has a vocalCrowd flag set, meaning that audience reactions are amplified in the simulation — a Chicago Show crowd is loud, opinionated, and will let a worker know when they have under-delivered.

Chicago Show also has the storylinesExpected flag set, which means the simulation will penalize the promotion if it books shows without ongoing storyline threads. This is a Sports Entertainment hallmark: the promotion cannot simply book "good matches" and expect to be rewarded; it must tell stories that connect one show to the next.

Strategic Position

Chicago Show enters 2025 in a paradoxical position: financially dominant, culturally marginal. The promotion has more cash than every other promotion combined, but it has zero prestige, zero regional popularity outside North America (where it sits at a modest 40), and no allies. It is the only American promotion in a world where the action is happening in Tokyo and Mexico City.

The strategic question for any player booking Chicago Show is whether to double down on the Sports Entertainment identity that has kept the promotion alive for 75 years, or to pivot toward a more workrate-driven style that could compete with the Japanese and Mexican promotions on their own terms. The first path is safe but may perpetuate the prestige gap; the second is risky and could alienate the existing fanbase, but could also unlock the international markets that Chicago Show currently cannot reach.

The promotion's most valuable asset is not its money or its history — it is its isolation. Chicago Show is the only promotion that can credibly book shows in North America without direct competition. If the player chooses to expand into Canada (where regional popularity scores for wrestling reach as high as 90 in Ontario) or the Caribbean, there is no incumbent promotion to fight. The continental vacuum is Chicago Show's to claim, if it can find the workers and the will to claim it.

History

Lucha Bellas was founded on January 1, 1980 — the same founding date as its Mexican sibling-rival Lucha MEX, a coincidence that has fueled twenty-five years of mutual resentment. The promotion was created as a women's-exclusive platform for Lucha Libre, the high-flying, mask-driven style that is Mexico's most recognizable wrestling export. Where Lucha MEX represents the older, more conservative tradition of Mexican wrestling, Lucha Bellas was founded explicitly to push the boundaries of what women's wrestling could be in a country where it had been treated as a novelty for decades.

The promotion's name — Lucha Bellas, "the beautiful fighters" — is itself a statement. It is a deliberate play on the tension between aesthetic and athleticism that defines women's wrestling in Mexico. The promotion does not deny that its workers are celebrated for their appearance as much as their workrate; it embraces it. The result is a promotion where masks, costumes, and character work are at least as important as in-ring ability, and where a worker's gimmick can carry her further than her moveset.

Despite this, Lucha Bellas enters 2025 with a regional popularity score of zero across every continent — including North America, the continent it is based in. This is the deepest weakness in the promotion's structural position. The promotion has been running for forty-five years but has, according to the world database, no measurable fanbase anywhere on Earth. Whether this reflects a failure of historical booking, a deliberate clean-slate design choice by the modder, or simply a placeholder that will be filled in later is one of the open questions of the BETA world.

Style & Presentation

Lucha Bellas practices Lucha Libre, one of the most distinctive styles in professional wrestling. The style is defined by its emphasis on aerial offense, mask-based character identities, and a structured code of honor regarding masks (in real-world lucha libre traditions, losing one's mask in a "mask vs. mask" match is the most devastating loss a worker can suffer). The promotion's entToWresRatio of 5 is exactly at the world default, indicating a balanced roster — workers who can fly, workers who can talk, and workers who can work holds.

The promotion's impact, danger, and violence ratings are all at the world default of 5, meaning Lucha Bellas is not an extreme or hardcore product. It is a wrestling promotion that prioritizes the work itself over the spectacle around it, even as it celebrates the costumes and characters that the lucha tradition demands. The vocalCrowd flag is set, meaning a Lucha Bellas crowd is loud and engaged — a hallmark of Mexican wrestling crowds, which are among the most passionate in the world.

Notably, Lucha Bellas does not have the storylinesExpected flag set, in contrast to Chicago Show and the two Japanese men's promotions. This means the promotion is not penalized for booking shows without ongoing storylines — a Lucha Bellas card can be a pure exhibition of athletic spectacle without narrative connective tissue, and the simulation will reward it. This is consistent with the lucha libre tradition, where matches are often self-contained athletic contests rather than chapters in an ongoing story.

Lucha Bellas hosts one annual signature event: Luchastica, scheduled for January Week 5 every year. The choice of January Week 5 for Luchastica is significant. It places the event at the very start of the ModzVerse canon calendar — the first major event of the year — and pits it directly against RAIBARAZU's Battle of Rivals supershow, which is also scheduled for January Week 5. A fan in Tokyo cannot attend both; a fan in Mexico City cannot attend both. The two supershows are fighting for the same calendar slot, and the simulation will track which one draws better. Strategic Position

Lucha Bellas enters 2025 as the most strategically vulnerable promotion in the ModzVerse. It has zero money, zero prestige, zero regional popularity across every continent, and zero allies. It is one of two promotions competing for the same Mexican fanbase, and its direct rival — Lucha MEX — is also at zero across the board, meaning the Mexican wrestling market is, in the database's eyes, entirely up for grabs. The first promotion to build a measurable fanbase will have a structural advantage that may be difficult to overcome.

The promotion's primary asset is its identity. Lucha Bellas is the only women's-exclusive lucha libre promotion in the world, and the only promotion with the cultural credibility to host a Luchastica-scale event. If the promotion can convert that identity into regional popularity — particularly in North America (where Mexican wrestling has deep roots) and South America (where lucha libre has historical influence) — it can establish a foothold that Lucha MEX cannot easily dislodge.

The Mask Question
The single most important booking decision a Lucha Bellas player will make is the mask. In lucha libre, a worker's mask is their identity, their gimmick, and their merchandise — and the moment they lose it (in a "hair vs. mask" or "mask vs. mask" match) is the most dramatic moment of their career. The ModzVerse supports this through the isMasked flag on workers. A Lucha Bellas roster without at least one or two masked workers with significant storyline investment in their masks is not really a lucha libre roster at all.

History

Lucha MEX shares its founding date — January 1, 1980 — with Lucha Bellas, a coincidence that the world's kayfabe history treats as no coincidence at all. The two promotions were born from the same split: a disagreement over the future of Mexican wrestling that pitted the old-school traditionalists against the new-wave mask-and-flash crowd. Lucha MEX is the traditionalist side of that split — the promotion that refused to embrace the mask-as-character tradition and insisted that Mexican wrestling should be about workrate, storytelling in the ring, and the older territorial style that predates the lucha libre explosion.

The promotion's name — Lucha MEX — is a direct assertion of national identity. Where Lucha Bellas chose a name about beauty and the workers themselves, Lucha MEX chose a name about the country. The promotion positions itself as the Mexican wrestling promotion, the keeper of the tradition, the place where wrestling is wrestling. The result is a promotion that is philosophically opposed to its sibling-rival on every axis: mixed-gender vs. women-exclusive, Traditional vs. Lucha Libre, workrate-driven vs. character-driven.

Despite the philosophical opposition, Lucha MEX enters 2025 with a regional popularity profile that is, in some ways, more respectable than Lucha Bellas'. The promotion has a North American popularity of 40 — matching Chicago Show — suggesting that despite its Mexican base, it has built a meaningful fanbase across the broader North American continent. Why this popularity score exists for Lucha MEX but not for Lucha Bellas is one of the mysteries of the ModzVerse database; the most likely explanation is that Lucha MEX's mixed-gender roster has historically drawn a broader audience than Lucha Bellas' women-exclusive product.

Style & Presentation

Lucha MEX practices Traditional wrestling — the catch-all style for promotions that do not commit to any of the more specialized styles. Traditional wrestling is, in the ModzVerse, the style of old-school territorial wrestling: chain wrestling, mat work, psychology-driven matches, minimal flash. The promotion's entToWresRatio of 6 is slightly above the world default, indicating a roster that can carry angles when needed but is primarily built for in-ring work.

What makes Lucha MEX distinct is its danger rating of 8 — the highest danger rating of any promotion in the ModzVerse. This means Lucha MEX workers are pushed harder, take more risks, and are more likely to be injured in the simulation than workers at any other promotion. This is consistent with the traditional Mexican wrestling style, which historically has been more physically demanding than the theatrical lucha libre style. The promotion's impact rating of 7 is also above average, indicating a hard-hitting product.

The promotion has both the vocalCrowd and storylinesExpected flags set. The storylines expectation is notable: Lucha MEX, despite being a Traditional promotion, is expected to book ongoing storylines — not just standalone matches. This places it closer to Chicago Show in structural expectations than to its sibling-rival Lucha Bellas, which is not penalized for booking storyline-free cards. A Lucha MEX player will need to think about ongoing narrative threads in a way that a Lucha Bellas player will not.

Strategic Position

Lucha MEX enters 2025 in a stronger structural position than its sibling-rival, despite having the same $0 in the bank and 0 prestige. The promotion's 40-point North American popularity is a meaningful asset — it is the second-highest regional popularity score for any promotion in the world (behind only RAIBARAZU's 60 in Asia). This gives Lucha MEX a real fanbase to build on, where Lucha Bellas is starting from absolute zero.

The promotion's primary strategic challenge is its identity. Lucha MEX is the "old school" Mexican wrestling promotion in a world that has largely moved past old-school Mexican wrestling. The danger=8 rating means the promotion's workers will be pushed to their physical limits, which is a double-edged sword: it produces compelling matches but generates injuries. The promotion's second strategic challenge is its lack of signature events. Without a focal point on the calendar, Lucha MEX has nothing to build storylines toward — and the simulation will penalize the promotion for booking shows without ongoing storylines (the storylinesExpected flag is set). The first Lucha MEX player will need to invent a supershow, give it a name, position it on the calendar, and start building toward it from week one.

History

RAIBARAZU — the Japanese transliteration of the English word rivals — was founded on June 6, 1950, just over five months after Chicago Show. The promotion was established in Tokyo, Japan, at the very beginning of the post-war Japanese wrestling boom, and was conceived from the start as a promotion built on internal competition. The name is the manifesto: Rivals. The company's founding premise is that the best wrestling is produced when workers are pushed to compete not just against opponents in the ring, but against their own stablemates for roster position, brand assignment, and top billing.

This premise was operationalized in the 1980s when RAIBARAZU formally split its roster into two competing brands: Doragon (Japanese for "Dragon") as the A-brand, and Fenikkusu (Japanese for "Phoenix") as the B-brand. The Doragon roster is the promotion's flagship — bigger shows, higher-profile matches, the workers the company wants to push as stars. The Fenikkusu roster is the developmental and undercard brand — younger workers, harder matches, smaller venues, and the constant pressure to earn a call-up to Doragon. The result is a promotion that is constantly producing intra-roster drama, as workers on each brand jockey for position and the company books inter-brand dream matches on its annual supershows.

RAIBARAZU enters 2025 as the largest promotion in the ModzVerse by every meaningful metric except money and prestige. It has the highest regional popularity score of any promotion (Asia: 60, dwarfing the next-highest score of 40 held by Chicago Show and Lucha MEX). It is the only promotion with two brands. It is the only promotion with the touringCompany flag set, meaning it is configured to run touring circuits in addition to its standalone shows. It is the only promotion with a drug testing policy in effect (testing for steroids, though not for soft or hard drugs). And it is the only promotion that runs three annual supershows — Battle of Rivals, Autumn Blood Moon, and Fall of Honor — each fed by a dedicated touring cycle.

The Two-Brand System: Doragon vs. Fenikkusu

RAIBARAZU is the only promotion in the ModzVerse with a brand split. The two brands — Doragon (A-brand) and Fenikkusu (B-brand) — are configured as separate roster units with separate importance levels (Doragon is the "A-Brand", Fenikkusu is the "B-Brand"), separate announce teams (currently empty, awaiting roster population), and separate logo assets (both currently using the placeholder logos.png file).

The Doragon-Fenikkusu dynamic is the engine of RAIBARAZU's booking. Workers on Doragon are pushed as the promotion's stars — bigger matches, higher-profile storylines, top billing on the three annual supershows. Workers on Fenikkusu are positioned as the underdogs — younger workers fighting for a call-up, veterans working stiff matches to prove they still belong, and the constant pressure of being one bad month away from being cut. The result is a promotion where every match has stakes beyond the match itself: a Doragon worker who loses to a Fenikkusu worker is being embarrassed; a Fenikkusu worker who beats a Doragon worker is making a case for promotion.

Inter-brand matches are reserved for the three annual supershows — Battle of Rivals (January), Autumn Blood Moon (September), and Fall of Honor (October). These are the only events where a Doragon worker will face a Fenikkusu worker, and they are the moments that define the promotion's annual narrative. A player booking RAIBARAZU will spend the entire year building toward these three shows, with each "Road to…" tour serving as the qualifying circuit that determines which workers earn the inter-brand spotlight.

The January Week 5 Bottleneck
Three of the world's biggest supershows — Battle of Rivals (RAIBARAZU), Luchastica (Lucha Bellas), and The AEGIS LEAGUE Finals (Valkyrie Project) — are all scheduled for January Week 5. This is the most crowded date on the ModzVerse calendar and the single biggest booking conflict of the canon year. A worker who is featured on one of these shows cannot appear on another; a fan who wants to attend cannot be in three places at once. The simulation will track viewership and attendance for all three events, and the loser of that three-way contest will start the canon year with a narrative deficit.

The RAIBARAZU Tour System Explained

RAIBARAZU is the only promotion in the ModzVerse that uses a touring-circuit model. The promotion runs three annual supershows — Battle of Rivals, Autumn Blood Moon, and Fall of Honor — and each is fed by a dedicated touring cycle in the weeks leading up to the show. The tour serves as the qualifying circuit: workers compete on tour stops to earn their spot on the supershow, and the results of the tour directly determine the card of the supershow.

The tour system is configured in the database with the recurrenceType='Tour' setting, distinct from the 'Annual' and 'Weekly' settings used by other events. Each tour is configured with repeatAnnually=1, meaning the same tour structure recurs year after year, and individual tour stops are pre-scheduled in the eventDates table with specific dates, preferred continents, preferred countries, and preferred regions.

The pre-scheduled tour dates in the ModzVerse database (38 entries in the eventDates table) show two complete tour cycles:

  • Road to Autumn Blood Moon — September 2 to September 27 (12 stops, every 2–3 days, all in Asia / Japan / countryID 137)
  • Road to Fall of Honor — October 1 to October 31, 2025 (13 stops, every 2–3 days, all in Asia / Japan / countryID 137)
  • Road to Frozen Combat — February 3 to February 28, 2025 (12 stops, every 2–3 days, all in Asia / Japan / countryID 137)

Notably, all tour stops are currently preferred for the same location — Asia / Japan. This is a BETA-era limitation; a fully-realized RAIBARAZU tour would likely include stops in other Asian countries (South Korea, Taiwan, the Philippines) and potentially international stops in North America and Europe as the promotion expands its reach.

The autobook configuration varies across the three tours. Road to Autumn Blood Moon and Road to Battle of Rivals have autobook=1, meaning the simulation will book them automatically. Road to Fall of Honor and Road to Frozen Combat have autobook=0, meaning the player must book them manually. This asymmetry is unusual and may be a BETA-era oversight; it suggests the modder intends for some tours to be self-running while others require player attention.

Event Profiles: By Promotion

The Chicago Show weekly event is the only weekly event in the ModzVerse. It airs every Sunday evening, runs for 60 minutes, and is configured with "Huge" importance — meaning every weekly show is treated by the simulation as a major event. In the ModzVerse, Chicago Show's weekly event is the supershow. There is no higher tier for the promotion to build toward.

The event has no preferred venue configuration, meaning the simulation will use the promotion's default venue (likely Chicago-area venues, once the venues table is populated). The event is not configured for autobooking, meaning the player must book every weekly show manually — a significant booking burden, since 52 shows per year is the highest booking volume of any promotion in the ModzVerse.

Titles & Brands

The ModzVerse contains exactly one championship and two brands — both belonging to RAIBARAZU. This is a deliberate scarcity. In a world where most wrestling simulations start with dozens of titles across multiple promotions, the ModzVerse has chosen to begin with a single championship, defended at only three events per year, and a single brand split owned by one promotion. The result is a world where championships and brands are precious — where the structural slots that exist are the only slots that will exist, at least until the canon year progresses and new titles can be created.

The Vacant Championship

As of January 1, 2025, the AEGIS LEAGUE championship is vacant. There is no reigning champion. The currentChampion, currentChampion2, and currentChampion3 fields are all empty strings, and the won date (which records when the current champion won the title) is set to January 1, 1980 — the title's creation date, not a recent victory. This is a BETA-era state: the title has no current holder because the worker roster is empty, and the first task of any Valkyrie Project player will be to fill the vacancy.

The vacancy presents both a challenge and an opportunity. The challenge is that a vacant title is, narratively, an awkward state — the simulation will not generate title-related storylines (defenses, contenders, rivalries) until a champion is crowned. The opportunity is that the first champion will be chosen by the player, not by historical accident. The decision of who to crown as the first AEGIS LEAGUE champion — and how to crown them — is one of the most consequential booking decisions in the entire ModzVerse.

The most likely scenario is a tournament. The Valkyrie Project's first canon event is Valhalla, scheduled for January Week 1 — the very first week of the year. A tournament final at Valhalla would crown the first AEGIS LEAGUE champion on the very first event of the canon calendar, immediately establishing the title's lineage and giving the promotion a focal point for the rest of the year. Alternatively, the player could stretch the vacancy across multiple events, building toward a tournament final at Twilight of The GODS in July or The AEGIS LEAGUE Finals in January Week 5 (the title's namesake event).

The Defendable Flag

The AEGIS LEAGUE has defendable=0, which is an unusual configuration. In the simulation, the defendable flag controls whether a title is subject to the standard "must defend within X days" rule. With defendable=0, the title does not have to be defended on any particular schedule — the champion can hold the title for as long as the player wants without facing automatic mandatory defenses.

Instead, the title is defended only at the three Valkyrie Project signature events: Valhalla (January Week 1), Twilight of The GODS (July Week 5), and The AEGIS LEAGUE Finals (January Week 5). This means the title has at most three defenses per year, and possibly fewer if the player chooses not to defend at one of the events. The result is a championship where each defense is a major event — a rare and significant moment in the world's narrative, rather than a weekly or monthly occurrence.

The alwaysDefend=0 flag confirms that the title does not have to be defended at every signature event. The player can choose to skip a defense window if the storyline does not call for one, building anticipation for the next defense. This is a deliberate design choice that makes the AEGIS LEAGUE feel like a special, carefully-curated championship rather than a routine title defended every month.

The Doragon-Fenikkusu Booking Model

A RAIBARAZU player booking the two-brand system will need to think about roster management in a fundamentally different way than a single-brand promotion. The Doragon roster should be smaller and more prestigious — perhaps 15–20 workers, all established stars, with clear main event, upper midcard, and midcard tiers. The Fenikkusu roster should be larger and more developmental — perhaps 20–30 workers, including young lions, veterans on the downswing, and workers recovering from injury or storyline setbacks.

The two rosters should rarely mix. Regular shows should be brand-exclusive — a Doragon show features only Doragon workers, a Fenikkusu show features only Fenikkusu workers. The only events where inter-brand matches occur are the three annual supershows (Battle of Rivals, Autumn Blood Moon, Fall of Honor), where the dream-match dynamics of Doragon vs. Fenikkusu are the headline attraction. This booking model creates anticipation: a fan who wants to see a Doragon ace face a Fenikkusu up-and-comer must wait for the supershow, because it will not happen anywhere else.

The Call-Up
The single most dramatic moment in RAIBARAZU's annual booking is the call-up: the moment when a Fenikkusu worker is promoted to the Doragon roster. This is the equivalent of a minor-league baseball player being called up to the majors — a career-defining moment that signals the promotion's faith in the worker's future. A player booking RAIBARAZU should reserve call-ups for major moments: after a Fenikkusu worker wins a big match at a supershow, after a Doragon worker is injured and a replacement is needed, or after a storyline arc where a Fenikkusu worker has definitively proven they belong at the top.

Strategic Notes by Promotion Style

Different promotion styles favor different gimmicks. The ModzVerse's five active styles each have a distinct "gimmick palette" — a set of gimmicks that work well with the style's booking model and a set that do not.

Sports Entertainment (Chicago Show)

Chicago Show's high entToWresRatio of 7 means the promotion's roster is built around characters, not workrate. The ideal Chicago Show gimmicks are the entertainment-driven ones: Arrogant, Cocky, Millionaire, Royalty, Musical Performer, and Unstable. These gimmicks all have Talking Angle Boost or Video Angle Boost, which amplifies the entertainment value of the promotion's high-angle product. Gimmicks like Athlete (which requires wrestling skill and provides a match boost but no angle boost) are less valuable in Chicago Show, where matches are secondary to storylines.

Lucha Libre (Lucha Bellas)

Lucha Bellas' balanced entToWresRatio of 5 means the promotion can support both workrate-driven and character-driven gimmicks. The mask tradition of lucha libre favors certain gimmicks strongly: Monster (the intimidating masked heel), Supernatural (the mystical masked worker), Evil Foreigner (the foreign ruda who challenges the Mexican technico), and Underdog (the sympathetic small masked worker who fights from below). The Wholesome Face gimmick is also valuable for the technico side of the lucha dynamic.

Traditional (Lucha MEX)

Lucha MEX's workrate-driven style and high danger rating favor gimmicks that emphasize toughness and credibility: Athlete, Badass, Classic Heel, and Wholesome Face. Gimmicks that rely on entertainment skill (Arrogant, Cocky, Millionaire, Royalty) are less valuable here, because Lucha MEX's product is built around in-ring work, not angles. The Underdog gimmick can also work well, particularly for younger workers coming up through the Lucha MEX system.

Pure Wrestling (RAIBARAZU)

RAIBARAZU's low entToWresRatio of 3 means the promotion is the most sport-like in the ModzVerse. The ideal RAIBARAZU gimmicks are the ones that enhance in-ring credibility rather than entertainment: Athlete, Badass, Monster (for the giant heel archetype), and Classic Heel. The Pure Wrestling style does not reward gimmicks that depend on entertainment skill — workers with the Arrogant, Cocky, Millionaire, or Royalty gimmicks will feel out of place in a RAIBARAZU ring. The realSportsFeel aesthetic of the promotion demands workers who look like athletes, not characters.

Kings Road (Osaka Joshi, Valkyrie Project)

The two Kings Road promotions have different gimmick palettes despite sharing a style. Osaka Joshi, with its entToWresRatio of 7, can support the entertainment-driven gimmicks: Royalty, Arrogant, Cocky, and Unstable all work well for the character-driven women's wrestling product. Valkyrie Project, with its lower entToWresRatio of 4 and realSportsFeel flag, favors the more sport-like gimmicks: Athlete, Badass, Classic Heel, and Wholesome Face. The two promotions can use the same style but interpret it through fundamentally different gimmick choices.

Geography of the ModzVerse

The ModzVerse contains the most granular geographic dataset of any wrestling simulation world: 190 countries and 3,218 regions, each with its own population, economy, dictatorship status, wrestling popularity score, and preferred wrestling style. This is not just a backdrop — it is an active simulation layer that determines how much a promotion earns when it books a show in a given region, how much a worker's regional popularity grows when they win over a local crowd, and which styles of wrestling will resonate with which fanbases.

Every country on Earth is represented, from the largest (China, population 1.4 billion) to the smallest (Vatican City, population 800). Every region within those countries — every state in the United States, every prefecture in Japan, every province in Canada — has its own data row. The result is a world where a booking decision in Ontario, Canada (population 14.9 million, Strong Style preference, wrestling popularity 90) is mechanically distinct from a booking decision in Quebec (population 8.6 million, Lucha Libre preference, wrestling popularity 75), even though both are in the same country.

How to Use the Venue Packs

The venue packs are designed to be loaded into a ModzVerse save file individually or in combination. The recommended approach depends on the player's booking strategy:

  • Single-region promotion: Load only the regional pack matching the promotion's home continent. A Chicago Show player loads NorthAmerica; a RAIBARAZU player loads Asia; a hypothetical Australian Hardcore promotion loads Oceania. This keeps the venue list manageable (2,000 venues) and ensures every show is bookable locally.
  • Touring promotion: Load the regional packs for every continent the promotion tours. A RAIBARAZU player running international tours would load Asia (home), NorthAmerica (for US tours), Europe (for European tours), and Oceania (for Australian tours). This expands the venue list to 8,000+ but enables global touring.
  • Style-specific promotion: Load the style pack matching the promotion's style. A Hardcore promotion loads the Hardcore pack (332 venues); a Lucha Libre promotion loads the Lucha Libre pack (546); a Pure Wrestling promotion loads the Pure Wrestling pack (396). These venues will draw better for shows of that style than randomly-selected venues.
  • Capacity-matched promotion: Load the capacity pack matching the promotion's typical show size. A promotion running weekly TV shows loads the Medium pack (2,969 venues, 5,000-9,975 seats); a promotion running monthly supershows loads the Arena pack (2,095 venues, 20,000+ seats); an indie promotion running small shows loads the Small or Tiny pack (2,310 or 966 venues respectively).
  • Comprehensive load: Load all 18 packs. This gives access to every venue in the dataset (36,000 records, with overlaps deduplicated by venueID) but produces a very long venue picker list. Recommended only for players who want maximum booking flexibility.
⚠ Schema Version Note
All 18 venue pack databases are at schema version 103, while the original ModzVerse(BETA).db is at schema version 121. This means the venue packs are based on an older version of the world template. When loading a venue pack into a v121 save file, the simulation will run a migration to bring the venue pack's schema up to v121 — this is automatic in PWS but may take a few seconds. Do not be alarmed if you see migration messages in the console when loading a venue pack for the first time.
⚠ Other Tables in Venue Pack DBs
Each venue pack database contains more than just the venues table — they also include pre-populated promotions (5,000 entries), gimmicks (15,303 entries), titles (5,505 entries), and networks (101 entries) tables from the baseline template. These are not the canonical ModzVerse promotions/gimmicks/titles — they are template data that should be ignored or overwritten when merging the venue pack into a ModzVerse save file. Only the venues table should be imported; the other tables in the venue pack DBs are scaffolding.

The Roadmap to v1.0

The path from BETA to a fully playable v1.0 requires populating the empty tables listed above. The recommended order of operations, based on the dependencies between tables, is as follows:

Phase 1: Roster Foundation

The first and most important phase is populating the workers table. Without workers, no other table can be meaningfully populated — contracts need workers, venues need workers to perform in them, networks need workers to broadcast, tag teams need workers to team up, stables need workers to lead, and storylines need workers to feud. The recommended roster sizes are:

  • Chicago Show — 20-30 workers (mixed gender, entertainment-focused)
  • Lucha Bellas — 15-25 workers (all women, lucha libre specialists)
  • Lucha MEX — 20-30 workers (mixed gender, traditional workrate)
  • RAIBARAZU — 30-50 workers (all men, split between Doragon and Fenikkusu)
  • Osaka Joshi — 15-25 workers (all women, Kings Road)
  • Valkyrie Project — 12-20 workers (all women, Kings Road sport-focused)

Total: approximately 112-180 workers across the six promotions. Each worker requires the full 149-column worker schema (wrestling skill, entertainment, sex appeal, star power, intimidation, all six body conditions, six regional pops, and ~80 boolean trait flags).

Phase 2: Contracts & Announce Teams

Once workers exist, they need to be assigned to promotions via the contracts table. Each worker-promotion pair requires a contract with terms (Written vs PPA, monthly value or per-appearance fee, signing bonus, max appearances, merch percentage, allow-indy, iron-clad, no-house-shows). The announce teams for each promotion (4 slots per promotion, 24 total) also need to be filled — these are typically retired workers or non-wrestling personalities.

Phase 3: Venues & Networks

With rosters in place, the venues table needs to be populated with the physical locations where events are held. Each promotion needs at least one home venue, and the larger promotions need regional venues for touring. The networks table needs to be populated with the broadcast partners that air the events — each network has its own viewership, region, and deal terms.

Phase 4: Match Content

The matchgimmicks table needs the match type definitions (singles, tag, triple threat, ladder, cage, Royal Rumble, etc.). The matchstories table needs the play-by-play text beats that the simulation uses to generate match commentary. The angleTemplates and templateBeats tables need the pre-built angle scenes that the simulation uses to generate non-match segments.

Phase 5: Storyline Structures

The tagteams, stables, and storylines tables need to be populated with the ongoing narrative structures that give the world its initial dramatic texture. Who is feuding with whom? Which workers are teamed up? Which stables exist, and who leads them? This is the layer that turns a roster of workers into a living wrestling world.

Phase 6: Polish & Launch

The final phase involves populating the trainingSchools table (developmental territories for each promotion), the futureTriggerEvents table (scheduled storyline beats that will fire on specific dates), and the workerpictures / picturePool tables (visual assets for workers and promotions). Once these are in place, the world is ready for v1.0 launch.

Database Version 121
The ModzVerse BETA database is at schema version 121, which is newer than the version 70+ era referenced in the canonical PWS codebase documentation. This means the ModzVerse is using a more advanced schema than the baseline PWS simulation, and some features (such as advanced regional popularity tracking, which is currently disabled in the world header) may be available but turned off. A modder working with this database should verify that their PWS build supports schema version 121 before attempting to load the world.

Known Issues & Open Questions

The BETA world has several known issues and open questions that the modder should be aware of:

  • Lucha MEX and Osaka Joshi have no signature events. Both promotions have non-zero popularity but no scheduled supershows. This is a significant gap that needs to be addressed before v1.0.
  • The Road to Battle of Rivals tour has no pre-scheduled dates. Unlike the other RAIBARAZU tours, this one has no entries in the eventDates table. Either the tour is dynamically scheduled, or the dates are a BETA-era gap.
  • The autobook configuration is inconsistent across RAIBARAZU tours. Two tours are autobooked, two are not. This asymmetry may be intentional or may be an oversight.
  • Multiple promotions use the placeholder logos.png logo file. Lucha MEX, RAIBARAZU, Osaka Joshi, and Valkyrie Project all reference logos.png as their logo. This should be replaced with promotion-specific logo files before v1.0.
  • The AEGIS LEAGUE title image is the placeholder titles.png. A unique title image should be created for the only championship in the world.
  • The pr_plugin_data table contains a session token from a plugin run. This suggests a plugin has been run against the database at least once. The modder should be aware of this when sharing the database.
  • Several promotional description fields are empty. The promotions table has a description column, but all six promotions have empty descriptions. The lore in this wiki is the most substantial narrative content for the promotions; the database itself has no in-world descriptions.
